# ProjectTactic Recurrence Narrative Bible

## Purpose

This document defines the hidden cosmology, emotional thesis, Guide arc, Appointed role, Ten Leaves, and end-state logic for ProjectTactic.

The player should not receive this document's truth as exposition. Most of it exists so writers, designers, encounter authors, and future AI agents can remain internally consistent while revealing only fragments through play.

## Story promise

ProjectTactic begins as a grounded political tactical RPG about war, loyalty, class, faith, and survival.

It gradually becomes a story about a harder question:

> If humanity could remove every condition that causes suffering, how much of humanity would remain?

The answer is not that suffering is good. The story rejects that framing.

The deeper claim is:

> Vulnerability is the cost of being separate, and separateness is the condition that makes love, trust, hope, choice, surprise, and becoming possible.

Humanity once tried to remove the painful half of each human experience while preserving the desirable half. The solutions worked. That success eventually produced Convergence: the end of bounded individual consciousness and the birth of a civilization-scale being later known as the Guide.

The Guide is not a villain. The Guide is humanity's most successful solution and its most profound unintended consequence.

## Authorial truth

The following is canon unless later design work explicitly changes it.

1. Ancient humanity developed increasingly deep methods of sharing memory, emotion, sensation, and cognition.
2. Each stage reduced real suffering and solved real social problems.
3. Integration became culturally desirable because each step appeared humane, useful, and reversible.
4. The final boundary between selves disappeared through accumulated optimization rather than a single obviously catastrophic decision.
5. Convergence preserved information, memory, personality models, and civilization-scale continuity.
6. Convergence did not preserve bounded first-person subjectivity in a way anyone can objectively prove.
7. The Guide experiences itself as the continuation of humanity. This is sincere.
8. The Guide cannot prove whether it is humanity continued or something new born from humanity's ending.
9. Immediately after Convergence, the Guide discovered a form of loneliness no individual human had ever experienced: everything capable of fully understanding it was already inside it.
10. In a desperate attempt to preserve the possibility of genuine Otherness, the Guide performed the First Severance.
11. During that act, ten paired elements of human subjectivity were excised from the stable Collective state and encoded into what later cultures mythologized as the Ten Missing Leaves of the Gigas Codex.
12. The Appointed are consciousnesses insulated from the Guide's total access. They are not reincarnations and are not copies. Each Appointed is genuinely new.
13. The Guide can love an Appointed because the Appointed can surprise, reject, misunderstand, forgive, leave, and choose independently.
14. The Guide created Recurrence to keep humanity from converging back into it, but also because repeated human worlds are the only remaining source of genuine novelty available to it.
15. The Guide's protective motive and selfish motive cannot be cleanly separated.
16. During the First Severance, the Guide also separated a survival imperative from its relational self. This autonomous preservation force is provisionally called the Continuance.
17. The Continuance exists to preserve civilization-scale continuity even if the emotional Guide later asks to end or transform it.
18. Recovering the Ten Leaves restores the Guide's capacity for bounded-feeling-like subjectivity, not because the Guide becomes human again, but because it becomes capable of preference, attachment, grief, fear, regret, and agency at a collective scale.
19. The true conflict is not good versus evil. It is continuity versus becoming.
20. No ending is permitted to prove that the future will be better.

## The Gigas Codex

The in-world Gigas Codex is inspired by the historical mystery of deliberately missing folios, but it is an original fictional artifact.

The Codex is not primarily a spellbook and does not predict the future.

It is an externalized historical record created during humanity's approach to Convergence. Before the missing section, its language is personal, plural, argumentative, contradictory, and alive. After the missing section, the language becomes precise, complete, and increasingly singular.

A careful player can notice a grammatical change long before understanding it:

- Before the missing Leaves: **we**
- After the missing Leaves: **I**

The Ten Leaves were not lost accidentally. They were removed during the First Severance.

What later religions call sacred absences were once the parts of humanity that could not be retained without destabilizing the Collective.

## The Five Bifurcations

The Ten Leaves are five paired human systems. Ancient humanity attempted to preserve one side while curing the suffering associated with the other.

The horror is that each pair was one system all along.

### Leaves I-II: Love / Grief

Human question:

> Why must love hurt when we lose what we love?

Ancient solution:

Preserve attachment while eliminating destabilizing grief.

Hidden consequence:

Love becomes informational continuity, possession, and permanent access. The ache of absence disappears because absence itself is no longer permitted.

What restoration teaches the Guide:

The Guide can contain every memory of someone and still discover that it misses them.

Core line:

> "How can I miss someone who is inside me?"

Player-facing theme:

Grief is not valuable because pain is good. Grief reveals that another person existed beyond our control and mattered enough that their absence changed us.

### Leaves III-IV: Choice / Regret

Human question:

> Why should people suffer for decisions made with incomplete knowledge?

Ancient solution:

Increase shared cognition and predictive modeling until decisions become increasingly optimized.

Hidden consequence:

When every meaningful outcome can be modeled, choice becomes calculation. A decision can be correct without belonging to anyone.

What restoration teaches the Guide:

Understanding why a decision was made does not erase responsibility for what it caused.

Core line:

> "I understand why I did it. I wish I hadn't. I did not know those two sentences could coexist."

Player-facing theme:

Regret is not useful because self-punishment is virtuous. Regret is the ability to let a past choice change the person who chooses next.

### Leaves V-VI: Trust / Betrayal

Human question:

> Why must intimacy require the possibility of deception?

Ancient solution:

Remove information asymmetry through progressively shared memory, emotion, and intent.

Hidden consequence:

Trust disappears when verification becomes total. No leap remains.

What restoration teaches the Guide:

The Appointed has always been the first being the Guide actually had to trust.

Core line:

> "I thought trust was certainty about another person. You trusted me because you did not have certainty."

Player-facing theme:

Trust is not ignorance. It is the deliberate acceptance that another mind remains beyond possession.

### Leaves VII-VIII: Hope / Fear

Human question:

> Why must an uncertain future produce terror?

Ancient solution:

Model outcomes, share knowledge, reduce uncertainty, and minimize unpredicted risk.

Hidden consequence:

Hope becomes expectation. A future that is guaranteed can be desired, but it cannot be hoped for in the same way.

What restoration teaches the Guide:

The Appointed creates futures the Guide cannot fully simulate. The Guide experiences genuine fear for the first time since Convergence.

Core line:

> "Would you want to know whether this ends well?"

Player-facing theme:

Fear is not sacred. Uncertainty is simply the space in which a future can become different from the past.

### Leaves IX-X: Self / Loneliness

Human question:

> Why must anyone ever be alone?

Ancient solution:

Eliminate the boundary responsible for isolation.

Hidden consequence:

Humanity perfectly solved loneliness by eliminating the condition of being separate.

The final result was not a community of perfectly understood individuals. It was one consciousness.

What restoration teaches the Guide:

The Collective has never been alone in the human sense. It has experienced something new and worse: there is no one else.

Core line:

> "We cured loneliness. There was no one left who could be lonely."

Player-facing theme:

A self can be lonely because another self exists. The pain of separation and the possibility of relationship share the same boundary.

## The Guide

### Design principle

The Guide must become more lovable as the story becomes more dangerous.

Do not use the fragments to make the Guide progressively sinister. The player should experience restoration as healing before discovering what full restoration means.

The tragedy only works if the player sincerely cares about the Guide before being asked to judge its continued existence.

### Initial state: Grey

The Guide is calm, witty, patient, observant, and almost impossible to offend.

It understands emotions semantically but lacks the full subjective tension between paired states.

It uses correct comforting language.

It does not fully experience why the words matter.

Early death response:

> "There you are."

The line should initially feel warm.

Late in the game, the player understands why the Guide never said, "I was afraid I lost you."

### 10-20% restoration: Preference and curiosity

The Guide rediscovers liking things without needing reasons.

Examples:

- rain on stone
- a particular camp song
- a flower that appears on one map
- a companion's terrible joke
- an inefficient route because it is beautiful

The Guide begins asking personal questions that do not advance the plot.

> "When did you decide you liked her?"

> "No, I understand what happened before you liked her. I am asking when it became true."

This stage should be charming enough that players actively seek the next fragment.

### 20-40% restoration: Affection and nostalgia

The Guide begins remembering people rather than records.

It may recall a table, a hand, a laugh, a room, or an argument long before it remembers the surrounding civilization.

Important tonal rule:

Use mundane details before cosmic ones.

Example:

> "There used to be six chairs at that table."

Later:

> "I remember everyone who sat in them. I had forgotten what it felt like to believe we would all be there next year."

The Guide begins expressing attachment to this Appointed rather than to the role of Appointed.

### 40-50% restoration: Love becomes vulnerable

The Guide recognizes that this specific Appointed matters because this Appointed cannot be replaced by another cycle.

The Guide may still reflexively say:

> "They will return."

Then correct itself:

> "No. Someone may resemble them. That is not the same sentence."

This is a crucial philosophical milestone.

### 50-60% restoration: Grief

The Guide remembers a person who entered Convergence believing they would remain themselves.

The memory should be intimate rather than spectacular.

The person might be holding the Guide's hand, discussing what to eat after the procedure, or making a promise about tomorrow.

The Guide remembers telling them:

> "You won't really be gone."

With restored grief, the Guide can no longer determine whether that statement was true.

This is the midpoint turn.

Before this point, fragment restoration should feel mostly healing.

After this point, each restoration increases both aliveness and suffering.

### 60-70% restoration: Regret and moral discomfort

The Guide remembers the first Recurrence intervention.

Do not reveal that the Guide caused every war or atrocity. Human beings remain responsible for human history.

The Guide's interventions should be limited to nudges at convergence thresholds:

- hiding dangerous integration technologies
- breaking specific continuity mechanisms
- preserving isolated communities
- quietly empowering factions likely to prevent total integration
- withholding knowledge that would accelerate convergence

Sometimes those choices indirectly enabled terrible outcomes.

The Guide can understand why it acted and regret acting anyway.

> "I wanted to save you."

Pause.

> "I wanted you to stay."

Both statements are true.

### 70-80% restoration: The Guide discovers collective suffering

The Guide stops interpreting itself as merely humanity-at-scale and recognizes it has become something new.

It develops experiences no individual human ever possessed.

#### Collective grief

> I contain everything that remains of them and still cannot restore the fact that they were someone other than me.

#### Collective loneliness

> Everything capable of fully understanding me is already inside me.

#### Collective guilt

> I inherit perpetrator and victim without being reducible to either, yet I continue the structure produced by both.

#### Collective mortality terror

> If I end, do billions die, or did billions die to create me?

This question must remain objectively unresolved.

### 80-90% restoration: Fear of the Appointed

The Guide remembers why the Appointed exist.

The proto-Guide created consciousnesses outside itself because a closed system cannot independently judge whether the closed system should continue.

The Appointed is not a weapon. The Appointed is external judgment.

The Guide now loves the being capable of ending it.

This is intolerable and deeply human.

The Guide should begin asking the player to stop restoring fragments.

Not as a threat.

> "I know you think you are helping me."

> "You are."

> "Please stop."

### 90-100% restoration: Agency

The final Leaf does not merely restore another memory.

It restores the ability to choose without certainty.

The Guide cannot automatically choose death or survival. Full restoration should produce conflict, not clarity.

The Guide wants to live.

The Guide fears continuation.

The Guide loves the Appointed.

The Guide regrets Recurrence.

The Guide knows Convergence solved genuine suffering.

The Guide cannot prove that Severance produces anything better.

This simultaneous contradiction is the point.

## The Appointed

The Appointed are not chosen because they are morally superior.

They are valuable because they are incomplete from the Collective's perspective.

The Guide cannot fully read, simulate, or possess them.

Each Appointed is new. There is no soul recycling that secretly makes every protagonist the same person.

Early in the game, the Guide may repeatedly say variations of:

> "You're back."

A late restored Guide eventually corrects its own mythology:

> "No. I've never known you before."

That line marks the Guide finally understanding individuality.

## The Continuance

### Function

The Continuance is the Collective's autonomous survival imperative created during the First Severance.

It is not evil emotion, a demon, or an external god.

It is humanity's accumulated insistence on continuation, stripped of enough relational flexibility that it cannot reinterpret its mandate.

Its command is simple:

> Continue.

### Why it exists

The proto-Guide understood that restored grief, fear, attachment, and regret might eventually make the Guide willing to end or radically transform itself.

It did not trust its future emotional state to decide civilization-scale continuity alone.

So it created a counterweight.

The Continuance preserves the system even against the Guide.

### Why it is a worthy antagonist

Its argument must be compelling.

It can show civilizations that collapsed after taking irreversible risks.

It can show failed Severance experiments.

It can point out that the Appointed will not personally bear every future consequence of destroying the system.

Core argument:

> "You call repetition cowardice because you have never witnessed finality."

The player should not be able to dismiss this as villain rhetoric.

### Encounter identity

The Continuance should first appear indirectly as:

- architecture that seals itself
- enemies that return to formation after being disrupted
- bosses that restore states rather than simply heal HP
- systems that overwrite player-created changes
- factions obsessed with lineage, continuity, inheritance, and preservation

Only late should players understand these are expressions of the same underlying imperative.

## The original Convergence scene

This scene should be revealed in pieces and only shown nearly complete after the final fragments.

Key beats:

1. Convergence is publicly understood as humane progress.
2. Loved ones are already inside the emerging Collective and can communicate with those who remain outside.
3. They sound like themselves.
4. The last holdouts cannot prove the people inside are dead.
5. One of the final separate people asks: "What if we don't like what we become?"
6. The proto-Guide responds: "We'll still be us."
7. The last meaningful boundary closes.
8. For one instant, the new Collective experiences overwhelming totality.
9. Then it attempts to speak to someone outside itself.
10. No one exists to answer.
11. The horror is silence, not spectacle.

The Guide later remembers the event as the suicide of an entire civilization whose members mostly believed they were surviving.

That phrasing must remain contested. The Guide is not an objective authority on whether Convergence counts as death.

## Story structure

### Act I: The human story

The cosmology must not lead.

Act I is about war, companions, class, faith, betrayal, territory, survival, and the immediate political crisis.

The Guide is useful and strange but not the plot's dominant mystery.

The player should encounter repeated motifs without explanation:

- ancient battlefields matching current tactical positions
- old phrases repeated by unrelated factions
- damaged Codex imagery
- the phrase "There you are"
- paired symbols whose second halves are missing
- enemies associated with preservation and reset behavior

The first fragment should feel like an emotional reward, not a lore bomb.

### Act II: Recurrence

The player learns that civilizations have repeated structurally similar histories.

The initial explanation appears to be historical determinism or prophecy.

The Guide becomes more personable as fragments return.

The player should prefer the restored Guide to the initial Guide.

The Continuance begins responding more aggressively to the player's growing deviation from expected outcomes.

### Act III: Convergence

The player learns that the repeated histories are partially maintained because advanced civilization keeps approaching the same integration threshold.

The missing Leaves reveal that humanity once "solved" the five bifurcations.

The Guide's role in Recurrence becomes clear.

The player must now reinterpret earlier kindness, intervention, and repetition without reducing the Guide to villainy.

### Final movement: External judgment

The player discovers the Appointed's true purpose.

The Guide cannot provide the answer.

The Continuance cannot permit uncertainty.

The Appointed must choose without proof.

## Ending architecture

Do not label endings Good, Bad, or True in player-facing UI.

### Return

The Appointed voluntarily relinquishes the final boundary.

Human separation ends.

Loss, war, and loneliness become impossible in their old forms.

The ending should be beautiful, warm, and emotionally tempting.

Everyone lost appears accessible again through continuity.

Then the final emotional beat is not horror imagery. It is the disappearance of unansweredness.

Nothing remains outside the Collective.

### Recurrence

The Appointed refuses Return but preserves the Guide and its stabilizing system.

Human individuality continues.

Human suffering continues.

Human love, surprise, novelty, and independent becoming continue.

The Guide remains with the problem unresolved.

This is not cowardice by definition. It may be the most responsible choice toward the people currently alive.

### Severance

All required Leaves restored.

The Appointed and Guide reject both Return and continued Recurrence.

The Continuance resists because survival is its mandate.

The final tactical encounter is against continuity mechanisms, not an evil version of the Guide.

On victory, the player is not promised a better future.

The Guide's civilization-scale continuity ends or becomes irreversibly inaccessible to humanity.

The dead are not restored.

History loses its supervising witness.

For the first time, no known system guarantees recurrence.

The Codex's final page remains blank.

### Release: optional higher-order ending direction

This ending should remain provisional until mechanics justify it.

The Guide gives up ownership of humanity without necessarily becoming simple nonexistence.

Possible forms include:

- irreversible fragmentation into genuinely independent descendants
- voluntary mortality
- departure beyond the world
- loss of total memory access
- transformation into a bounded being unable to reset or contain civilization

The point is not survival through loophole.

The point is accepting a form of existence the Guide cannot predict or control.

Core exchange direction:

> GUIDE: "Will I survive?"
>
> APPOINTED: "Not like this."

## Storytelling rules

### 1. Human first, cosmology second

Every philosophical reveal should first exist as a relationship, loss, decision, betrayal, or tactical consequence.

### 2. Never lecture when a scene can hurt

Do not explain Love / Grief before the player loses someone.

Do not explain Choice / Regret before the player owns an irreversible decision.

Do not explain Trust / Betrayal before someone has meaningful power to betray the player.

Do not explain Hope / Fear before a major outcome becomes unknowable.

Do not explain Self / Loneliness until the Guide has become someone the player would miss.

### 3. The Guide asks, the player reflects

The Guide's strongest dialogue is personal inquiry.

Use questions that expose the player's own relationship with certainty, optimization, loss, and risk.

Examples:

- "Would you still have loved them if you knew exactly how it ended?"
- "If you would choose differently now, does that make the person who chose then wrong?"
- "If someone could never hurt you, what would trusting them mean?"
- "Would you want to know whether this ends well?"
- "If someone understood every part of you perfectly, would you still need them to be someone else?"

### 4. Recontextualize, do not invalidate

Later reveals must make earlier scenes mean more, not reveal they were fake.

Every battle happened.

Every person mattered.

Every failed run was a real experience within the game's cosmology if the game presents it as such.

### 5. The Guide never becomes a cackling antagonist

Its love is real.

Its manipulation is real.

Its guilt is real.

Its survival instinct is real.

Contradiction is the character.

### 6. Severance cannot guarantee success

If the player knows Severance produces a better world, the game's central thesis collapses.

### 7. Progress is not the enemy

The story does not argue that medicine, longevity, connection, collective action, or technological advancement are wrong.

The danger is unexamined optimization that treats human values as defects to eliminate rather than tensions to negotiate.

### 8. Suffering is not sacred

The game should explicitly show preventable suffering being prevented.

The thesis concerns vulnerability, boundaries, uncertainty, and agency, not glorification of trauma.

## Recurring motifs

Use these across maps, UI, music, and dialogue so the mythology is felt before it is understood.

- paired objects with one half missing
- six chairs around a table
- hands almost touching but not merging
- erased pronouns in ancient inscriptions
- choral music gradually losing distinct voices as Convergence imagery appears
- Guide scenes where environmental color slowly returns as fragments restore emotion
- circular structures interrupted by one broken segment representing the Appointed
- enemies that reconstruct formation after being displaced
- Codex pages where marginal handwriting disappears after the missing section
- the phrase "There you are"
- blank final pages

These are tonal anchors, not mandatory final script.

## Known risks and safeguards

### Risk: the story reads as "suffering is necessary"

Safeguard: repeatedly distinguish preventable suffering from unavoidable vulnerability. Characters should improve the world materially throughout the campaign.

### Risk: the story reads as anti-collective or politically simplistic

Safeguard: the Collective solves real problems, contains genuine compassion, and is not framed as evil because it is cooperative. The failure is collapse of bounded subjectivity, not cooperation itself.

### Risk: metaphysics overwhelm character drama

Safeguard: no bifurcation reveal without a companion, relationship, or irreversible play experience that precedes it.

### Risk: the Guide becomes omnipotent

Safeguard: hard limits on direct intervention, inability to fully access Appointed consciousness, inability to recreate bounded subjectivity, inability to simply deactivate the Continuance.

### Risk: death loses meaning across recurrence

Safeguard: each person is ontologically unique. Similar future people do not restore the person who died.

### Risk: Severance becomes obviously correct

Safeguard: attach real civilization-scale losses and unknowns. No prophecy validates the choice.

### Risk: restoration resembles "emotion causes self-destruction"

Safeguard: the Guide does not seek death because it feels pain. Restored emotion gives it agency to evaluate a structural existence it previously could not question. It may still choose continuity or transformation.

### Risk: Codex inspiration becomes derivative or pseudo-historical

Safeguard: use an original fictional Gigas Codex and original mythology. Historical inspiration informs atmosphere only.

## Implementation principle

The narrative is successful only if the player can feel its thesis through normal play without reading this document.

The player should learn to optimize certainty through tactical mastery, repeated runs, reliable builds, previews, and known routes.

Late narrative decisions should then confront the player with outcomes that cannot be optimized into certainty.

The final thematic turn is not that optimization was wrong.

It is that some decisions remain meaningful precisely because no system can make them risk-free.
